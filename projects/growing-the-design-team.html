Overview

I was a Design Manager at ArcTouch in Brazil, and later in Amsterdam. I managed 12 designers directly, both graphic (digital) and product designers, within a design team that grew from 20 to 26. I shared management of the wider team with another Design Manager, and supported recruiting them as the team grew. As a manager, I worked with internal and external stakeholders on the business side of design: client pitches, estimates, audits, staffing and planning.

The problem

Four things had to improve at the same time as the team grew:

  • Filling big shoes. The last design leader was a great cultural and technical reference. My transition happened when this person left, while the company was redesigning its management positions.
  • Hiring quality. The team was growing quickly, and every new hire had to be skilled and a good cultural fit. When new projects came in with no available staff, partner companies would also send us temporary workers.
  • Scale. Onboarding, documentation and knowledge-sharing depended too much on individual people. Supporting various clients and projects was a team-wide challenge.
  • Career growth clarity. Designers needed to know their gaps and what to work on next, and managers needed a fair way to defend promotions.

What I did

Career growth. I created structured career frameworks and mentoring programs, so each designer knew their gaps and what to work on next. I set clear expectations in three areas: visibility, ownership and documentation. Reviews and development plans moved into a shared tool, with 1:1s as a shared responsibility between manager and designer.

Hiring. I led portfolio reviews and interviews at every step of the process, and ran hiring calibration so the whole panel judged candidates the same way. Alignments between the other Design Manager and me made our requirements clearer, especially when we looked at the tech skills of each candidate.

Knowledge sharing. Optional weekly ceremonies kept the team connected between the various clients. They gave us space to share knowledge as a team, and informal mentorship started to happen. Designers with strong skills often presented 20-minute workshops with tips and “how-to’s” on Figma and other tools.

Design + Business. As the team understood the changes we were going through, it became easier for me as a leader to be present in their client meetings for strategic support and design critique. During the sales process, I was also on the tech call with the client’s teams to make sure our skills and ways of working were clear, while building connections with them. I had supported managers on pitches and estimates before becoming one, and several of those pitches became projects. As a manager, I owned the weekly staffing decisions and the estimates, which I reviewed at each contract renewal, every one to two months.

The outcome

Four designers were promoted to Senior in one year, onboarding and documentation improved, and the wider team grew from 20 to 26 designers while keeping a strong culture fit. Design delivery also lined up better with commercial opportunities, because designers and managers were involved earlier in pitches, estimates and staffing. Clients extended and returned because they valued our craft, and some kept the same designers on their products for years. Designers stayed an average of 2 years and a half.

Best shared in conversation

People decisions deserve context, so I keep the details off the page. I’m happy to walk through them in a meeting:

  • How the career frameworks were built, and how I defended promotions with them.
  • Hard calls on hiring, performance and team changes, and what I learned from them.
  • What I would do differently, and how I would run it again.
